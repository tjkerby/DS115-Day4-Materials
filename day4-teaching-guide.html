DS 115 Day 4: Teaching guide

Academic pathways and resources for getting started

Purpose

Day 4 explains how BYU’s data science pathways help students learn the field. Students do not need to know the professional process, have a settled career goal, or find the perfect major before they begin.

The lecture first explains the seven DS requirement areas. It then visits the five programs’ actual requirements and uses a Venn diagram to show shared foundations and different curricular depth. For students who are unsure, the instructor recommends beginning with the Data Science BS and considering a pivot as their interests develop. Their success in the field is unlikely to hinge on which affiliated major they ultimately choose.

The final section introduces resources for the graduation-plan assignment: the catalog, a sample MAP sheet, MyMAP, and academic advising. It includes brief demonstrations, rather than an in-class planning workshop.

Before class

  • Open the slides and the five linked program pages in separate tabs.
  • Open the supplied Data Science MAP sheet, MyMAP, and the CMS Advisement Center page.
  • Choose an appropriate view for classroom sharing during the MyMAP demonstration.
  • Review Canvas instructions for the graduation-plan and advisor/TA assignments so you can state the actual deadlines and submission requirements.
  • Check the current appointment options on the advisement page. If it is unavailable, the displayed phone and email provide a way to request an appointment.

A supportive opening

Slide 2 asks students to recall work they already saw in Day 3. Slide 5 explains how mathematics, statistics, and computing help them grow into that work. Explain the table yourself. Students should hear that the curriculum gives them time to develop these skills.

Avoid asking students to diagnose a process they have not learned or map an unfamiliar career to a major. Questions later in the lecture ask them to notice something in a catalog or diagram they have just seen.

The DS requirements

Slide 6 opens the actual DS catalog. Walk through the next seven slides in order.

Requirement Teaching emphasis
1: Introductory courses A starting vocabulary and basic tools
2: Mathematical foundations The structure behind models and computation
3: Statistical foundations Evidence, uncertainty, and modeling
4: Computing foundations Reliable programs and organized data
5: Data science core Courses that combine disciplinary perspectives
6: Electives and specialization Choices that can deepen theory, methods, or applications
7: Advanced theory and experience ML/DL theory as well as capstone, research, and internship options

Requirement 6 is broader than an applied-context label suggests. Requirement 7 includes advanced theory courses, so it should not sound as though every student must choose an experiential capstone. In both cases, the examples on the slides are a selection from the approved list, not complete degree rules.

Courses used in Requirement 7 cannot also count toward Requirement 6. Variable-credit research or internship choices still need the required credit amount and approvals. Some choices require prerequisites outside the major.

The catalog comparison and Venn diagram

Slide 15 previews three descriptive questions: what foundations overlap, where a major builds more depth, and where students have a choice. Slide 16 links the five programs.

Allow about eight minutes for the live tour. Scroll to Program Requirements and explain a few concrete observations on each page:

  1. Data Science: Recall the broad foundation and flexible elective block.
  2. Statistics, Data Science emphasis: Notice additional required statistical modeling and inference, plus its alternative culminating paths.
  3. Machine Learning: Notice software and algorithm courses and required advanced ML/DL.
  4. ACME: Look at the mathematical preparation, structured junior/senior core, and concentration.
  5. Applied Physics, Data Science emphasis: Look at the physics foundation, labs, and scientific research or culminating work.

Slide 17 includes STAT 121 in the shared foundation. Machine Learning allows STAT 201 instead, so the slide and diagram mark that alternative explicitly.

Slide 18 adapts the Venn diagram from the supplied older lecture. It preserves the original circles and updates their text. It shows selected core requirements, rather than every required course or all elective overlap:

  • Center: CS 111, CS 235, MATH 112, MATH 213, MATH 215, and STAT 121 with the ML alternative marked.
  • DS and Statistics: MATH 113 and STAT 230, 240, 251, and 330.
  • DS and ML: CS 240, 270, 404, and MATH 380.
  • Outer regions: selected examples of each program’s further core requirements.

An outer-region course can still be available as an elective in another major. For example, ML lists CS 452 and STAT 240 among elective choices. The Statistics program offers either the CS 482/483 capstone sequence or the STAT 386/486 sequence. For that reason, STAT 386 does not appear as an unconditional shared requirement in this diagram.

The current Statistics catalog lists STAT 430 rather than the older deck’s STAT 250. Introductory data science courses use different cross-listed prefixes across sources, so the selected diagram omits that course rather than suggesting separate content.

A low-pressure discussion prompt is: What did you notice about the overlap or a difference in the diagram? Students can simply refer to what is on the screen.

Major choice in perspective

Slide 19 gives undecided students a useful starting point: begin in DS, experience its broad foundations, and explore a related major later if their interests point that way. Students who already intend another major should use that major.

Slide 20 makes the instructor’s main point directly: majors matter, but students’ success or failure in data science is unlikely to be determined by which affiliated major they end up choosing. Their learning, projects, research, and work experience can shape their development beyond the required curriculum. This is teaching guidance, not a claim that all programs produce identical outcomes.

Early overlap often leaves room to pivot. An advisor can explain how a specific switch affects completed credit and future course sequencing. Avoid promising that every switch adds no coursework or delay.

A short introduction to planning resources

On slide 21, open the supplied Data Science MAP sheet. Show that it lists requirements and suggests which courses to take in each semester, including GE and religion courses. It provides substantial help getting started on the homework. Students should adapt the sample sequence to their prior credit, responsibilities, and own catalog year.

Slide 22 gives a real example of prerequisites through CS 111, CS 235, and CS 240. It is one accepted route, not a complete prerequisite map. CS 235 also lists CS 142 as an alternative, and CS 240 permits instructor consent.

Slide 23 condenses the scheduling exercise and answer into one slide. The courses are hypothetical. Assume no prior credit or summer courses and require each prerequisite to finish before the next course begins.

Start A First B First C
Fall, Year 1 Winter, Year 1 Winter, Year 2
Winter, Year 1 Fall, Year 2 Winter, Year 2
Fall, Year 2 Winter, Year 2 Winter, Year 3

Give students 30-45 seconds, then reveal the answer. The lesson is simply to check both prerequisites and offerings. There is no separate follow-up exercise.

On slide 24, demonstrate MyMAP for about three minutes. Show what it can help students do: see how credit applies, identify remaining requirements, and organize a tentative plan. Use current labels. Do not promise automatic checks or buttons that have not been verified.

On slide 25, open the CMS Advisement Center page and show the appointment options available there. Do not book an appointment during the demonstration. The slide also gives 801-422-2674 and cmsadvising@byu.edu as contact options. Students can seek help while their plans are still rough. An advisor can confirm degree rules and substitutions, while a TA can share course experiences and help students prepare questions.

Slide 26 connects these resources to the existing graduation-plan assignment. Keep its Canvas rubric, format, and due date. There is no separate worksheet, in-class planning period, or partner-review requirement in this lecture.

Exit ticket and conclusion

Slide 27 asks:

  1. What did you notice about the overlap among the majors?
  2. Name one course and something it will help you learn.
  3. Which resource will help you take your next step?

Students may remain undecided. Use the attendance quiz or a short reflection according to the course’s existing routine.

Slide 28 closes with encouragement to begin. An undecided student can start in DS with mathematics, programming, and statistics, notice what resonates, and refine the major choice through experience.

Source and catalog-year notes

The DS catalog URL supplied by the instructor points to the 2025-2026 edition. The lecture’s other four program links open the current catalog. The comparison is an instructional view of their overlapping foundations, not a matched-year degree audit. Students should use the catalog year attached to their own record for graduation decisions.

The archived DS catalog’s introductory block lists four courses plus one introductory data science course selected from two options. It avoids the incomplete introductory-block display encountered on the current public catalog. The slide uses DS 115 and DS 180 as current course labels. The supplied MAP sheet uses the older STAT 115 and CS 180 labels, which should be matched to the student’s applicable requirements.

Public web retrieval of the supplied DS URL and MAP link was intermittent. Indexed official BYU content supplied the archived requirements and the MAP sheet’s semester sequence. Open the pages before class to verify the live demonstration. The CMS Advisement Center destination is linked by BYU’s official Data Science site. Its phone and email are corroborated by the official CS advisement page.

The reference credit totals describe published major credits, not total university graduation credits or time to graduation. They should not serve as a ranking of rigor or career value.
